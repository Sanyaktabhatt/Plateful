Method

How the recipe gets written

01

The prompt

Every training recipe is rewritten into one fixed shape. The first line holds the ingredient names only, shuffled, with no amounts, because that’s what people actually type. Each section has a marker, so the output can be parsed back into a recipe and generation stops cleanly at <|end|>. At serving time the prompt ends at <|title|>: the model names the dish first, and that keeps the rest of the recipe on track.

<|recipe|>
<|ingredients|> lemon; chicken thighs; rosemary; garlic
<|title|> Lemon Rosemary Chicken
<|ingredient_list|>
- 2 lb. chicken thighs
- 4 cloves garlic, minced
<|directions|>
1. Heat oven to 425°. …
<|end|>

Grey: the prompt. Ink: what the model writes.

02

Fine-tuning GPT-2

GPT-2 base (124M parameters) from KerasHub, fine-tuned on recipes from RecipeNLG. Training runs in TensorFlow 2: Keras 3 on the TensorFlow backend, with tf.data input and AdamW on a linear-decay schedule.

03

Reading your list

A small BERT model tags each word you type as an amount, a unit or an ingredient. Only the ingredient names go into the prompt. Amounts are kept so they can be checked. RecipeNLG has no hand labels for this, so the tagger learns from labels a rule-based parser produces (weak supervision), with augmentation so it copes with bare names and lowercase.

The tagged names and the recipe title become a food-photography brief for SD-Turbo, a Stable Diffusion model distilled to run in two denoising steps.

04

The safety pass

Both your list and the generated recipe are checked, since the model often adds butter, flour or soy sauce you never mentioned.

  • Allergens. The US “Big 9” plus mustard, celery and sulphites from the EU list, with exceptions so coconut milk isn’t dairy and nutmeg isn’t a nut.
  • Impossible amounts. Zero or negative quantities, a cup of salt, 40 eggs, a teaspoon of chicken breast, 900°F ovens, 60-hour simmers. Each one is marked unusual or impossible.
